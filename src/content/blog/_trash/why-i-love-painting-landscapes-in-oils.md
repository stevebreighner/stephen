There is something deeply grounding about setting up a canvas and bringing a piece of the natural world onto a blank surface. For as long as I have been creating art, landscapes have been my primary source of inspiration, and oil paint has been the medium that lets me truly capture what it feels like to stand in those open spaces.

People often ask me why I stick with oils when there are faster, less messy options available. For me, it comes down to the unique physical qualities of the paint itself. I love its buttery texture, its rich depth of pigment, and above all, its slow drying time. Unlike acrylics, which dry almost immediately, oil paint stays workable for days. This allows me to take my time—blending soft edges in a morning sky, sculpting clouds, or fine-tuning the subtle color transitions across a distant hillside. It feels less like rushing against a clock and more like having a quiet conversation with the canvas.

Landscapes offer an endless variety of moods and atmospheric challenges. Light shifts constantly out in nature; shadows stretch, colors warm up as the sun dips, and distant trees soften into hazy blues and greens. Painting landscapes in oil gives me a way to pause those fleeting moments. My goal is never just to make a literal copy of a photograph, but rather to capture the feeling of being in that place—the warmth of late afternoon light, the cool stillness of a forest path, or the sweeping expanse of an open field.

I also love the versatility and texture that oil paints encourage. I can lay down thin, luminous glazes to suggest mist and water, or apply thick, expressive strokes with a palette knife to give real weight and structure to rocks and tree bark. That tactile quality makes the finished painting feel alive in a way that mirrors the richness of nature itself.

Ultimately, painting landscapes in oils is my favorite way to slow down and really observe the world around me. It keeps me connected to nature, and I hope that quiet, restorative energy comes through in every piece I share.
